Laparoscopic vs. Open Surgery & Contraindications

Advantages over Open Surgery ✓ Benefits

Why Choose a Minimally Invasive Approach?

  • 1. Reduced Surgical Trauma & Pain: 5–12 mm incisions avoid dividing large muscle bellies, decreasing postoperative opioid analgesia requirements.
  • 2. Preserved Pulmonary Function: Less upper abdominal pain enables deep inspiration and effective coughing, reducing basal atelectasis and pneumonia.
  • 3. Faster Return of Bowel Function: Reduced manual bowel handling and peritoneal desiccation decreases postoperative ileus and hospital stay.
  • 4. Lower Wound Morbidity: Lower rates of surgical site infection (SSI), wound dehiscence, incisional hernia, and long-term adhesions.
  • 5. Magnified High-Definition View: 10–15x optical magnification illuminates deep pelvic and subphrenic anatomical planes.
Downsides & Technical Limitations ⚠ Challenges

Technical & Physiological Trade-Offs

  • 1. Reduced Tactile Haptics: Inability to directly palpate subtle intraluminal masses or deep arterial pulses with bare fingers.
  • 2. The Fulcrum Effect: The abdominal wall trocar acts as a pivot point—moving the surgeon's hand right moves the internal instrument tip left.
  • 3. 2D Depth Perception & Restricted Wrist Motion: Monocular 2D screens require visual depth cues, and rigid shafts limit motion to 4 degrees of freedom.
  • 4. Physiology of CO₂ Pneumoperitoneum: Intra-abdominal pressure (12–15 mmHg) splints the diaphragm, compresses the IVC (reducing venous return/cardiac output), and causes systemic CO₂ absorption (hypercapnia/acidosis).

When Laparoscopic Surgery May Not Be Suitable

1. Absolute Contraindications
  • Hemodynamic Instability / Hemorrhagic Shock: Pneumoperitoneum further reduces venous return and delays rapid hemorrhage control.
  • Severe Cardiorespiratory Failure or Raised ICP: Inability to tolerate hypercapnia or IVC compression.
  • Uncorrectable Coagulopathy.
2. Relative Contraindications
  • Hostile / Frozen Abdomen: Dense pan-peritoneal adhesions from multiple prior laparotomies.
  • Massive Bowel Distension: High-grade bowel obstruction leaves no working space and risks trocar enterotomy.
  • Severe Bullous COPD.
3. Indications to Convert to Open
  • Failure to Progress / Unclear Anatomy: e.g., inability to achieve the Critical View of Safety in cholecystectomy.
  • Uncontrolled Hemorrhage or Visceral Injury.
  • Principle: Conversion is sound clinical judgment, never a failure.

Ergonomic Sweet Spot: A 60° manipulation angle with a 30° azimuth angle on either side of the optical axis minimizes instrument clashing and prevents shoulder fatigue.
The Baseball Diamond Rule

Optical & Instrument Triangulation

  • Manipulation Angle (α = 45°–75°, ideal 60°): Angle between left and right working instruments. Below 30° causes handle clashing ("sword-fighting"); above 90° forces extreme shoulder abduction.
  • Azimuth Angle (β = 15°–45°, ideal 30°): Angle between either working instrument and the optical axis of the laparoscope.
  • 1:1 Fulcrum Lever Ratio (15–20 cm to Target): Standard instruments are 30–35 cm long. Placing ports ~15 cm from the target keeps half the shaft inside and half outside.
Optical Physics

Why We Use a 30° Angled Laparoscope

  • Looking Over & Around Structures: Rotating the light cable post 180° shifts the view from looking down into the pelvis/Calot's triangle to looking up at the anterior abdominal wall.
  • Rule of the Light Lead: With a 30° scope, the direction of view points opposite the light cable post.

Entry Method 1

Hasson Open Cut-Down

Direct cut-down through skin, umbilical stalk/linea alba, and peritoneum under direct vision. Stay sutures anchor a blunt Hasson cannula. Virtually eliminates blind retroperitoneal great-vessel injury.

Entry Method 2

Veress Needle & Palmer's Point

Two distinct clicks (fascia, then peritoneum). Confirm with saline aspiration, hanging-drop test, and opening pressure < 8 mmHg. Use Palmer's Point (3 cm below left costal margin, mid-clavicular line) after NG decompression if a midline scar exists.

Entry Method 3

Optical Viewing Trocar

A 0° laparoscope inside a transparent conical tip allows real-time visual identification of each abdominal wall layer during entry. Popular in bariatric and upper-quadrant access.

Abdominal Wall Safe Zones, Vessels & Fascial Closure

  • Inferior Epigastric Vessels: Course deep to the rectus abdominis muscle (4–8 cm off midline). Place lateral working ports > 6 cm from midline (lateral to the linea semilunaris) under direct vision.
  • Superficial Vessels: Transilluminate the abdominal wall with the laparoscope before incising skin.
  • Ilioinguinal & Iliohypogastric Nerves: Keep lower quadrant trocars ≥ 2 cm superior and medial to the ASIS to prevent chronic nerve entrapment.
  • Fascial Closure Rule: Formally close the fascia of all ports ≥ 10 mm to prevent Richter's port-site hernia.
